# A testimony of possession outside the Muslim world

## Introduction

Jinn are part of Allah’s creation. Their existence does not depend on whether a society acknowledges them. Yet recognising the unseen and explaining a particular person’s suffering are different questions: faith gives us no licence to invent the details of an experience we have not witnessed.

The eighth episode of al-Sabeel’s series considers a woman’s account of frightening experiences in Oregon. Ahmad Dadoosh understands her story as an example of spiritual danger outside Muslim communities. His opening discussion challenges the suggestion that possession reports belong only to Muslim societies.[^e08-interview][^e08-qarin-plan]

The episode begins with a disagreement involving Tariq al-Suwaidan. Al-Suwaidan’s own website affirms the existence of jinn while disputing bodily possession. This distinction matters: disagreement about possession should not automatically be described as denial of jinn themselves. The historical arguments require their own examination; one person’s recollection of whom he met abroad cannot establish how common an experience is.[^e08-suwaidan][^e08-wasatiyya-date]

The same care applies to statistics. A Pew Research Center article cited in the discussion found that 22% of atheists held at least one of four New Age beliefs. Those questions concerned psychics, astrology, reincarnation and spiritual energy in objects. They did **not** measure belief in possession, and atheists, agnostics and people describing their religion as “nothing in particular” were separate groups.[^e08-pew]

## Kimberly’s story

### Reading the original account

Kimberly gave her account to *Soft White Underbelly*. The original interview’s complete English automatic captions have been read alongside al-Sabeel’s Arabic retelling. This allows comparison of what she says, although captions still need checking against the recording. Her testimony documents her account; it does not independently prove the accusations or the explanation she gives them.[^e08-interview]

She describes childhood abuse within her extended family, later efforts to build an independent life, and a housing crisis in 2018. After receiving notice to leave her home, she found a room in a house shared with other women. Her children stayed with their father during this period.[^e08-childhood][^e08-housing]

Two details need correction in the retelling. The original places the rental in **Medford**, while later identifying **Ashland** with the alleged group. It also distinguishes the homeowner from the person Kimberly describes as the group’s leader. Keeping these people and places separate is essential when discussing serious allegations.[^e08-housing][^e08-leader-identity]

Dadoosh interprets earlier abuse as a possible part of a later spiritual vulnerability. That proposed mechanism has not been established. A survivor’s history is a reason to listen with compassion, not grounds for treating that person as spiritually damaged or responsible for someone else’s abuse.[^e08-trauma-cause][^e08-fitra-abuse]

### Blue lotus and promises of spiritual knowledge

Kimberly describes the alleged leader as an “entheogenic historian”: someone presenting psychoactive substances in a religious or spiritual setting. She recalls encouragement to explore Egyptian imagery and supposed past lives.[^e08-entheogen-role][^e08-egyptian-flattery]

The discussion centres on a drink described as blue lotus. Its actual ingredients are unknown. When Kimberly later discusses possible added substances, she names alternatives; her words do not confirm the simultaneous DMT-and-LSD mixture described in the Arabic retelling.[^e08-drink]

Ancient symbolism also needs to be separated from modern claims. A Metropolitan Museum object description connects the Egyptian deity Nefertum with a lotus and creation imagery. This supports a historical religious association; it does not authenticate a modern group’s ritual or establish what was in a drink.[^e08-nefertum]

Nor does a plant’s spiritual reputation make its products harmless. An independently located case series describes five patients with altered mental status after reported blue-lotus product use. It supplies clinical context, not evidence for the episode’s unnamed studies of encounters with entities.[^e08-lotus-case-series][^e08-lotus-entity-studies]

The episode invokes the report prohibiting intoxicating and enervating substances. The wording appears in *Sunan Abi Dawud* 3686, but the edition checked displays al-Albani’s grading as weak. That grading should remain visible when this particular report is cited.[^e06-abudawud-mufattir]

## The group leader

### The experience and its aftermath

Kimberly says she became suspicious that drinks were being altered after experiencing weakness and numbness. This is her suspicion; no chemical test has been obtained to establish what she consumed.[^e08-coffee]

She describes a guided exercise involving an Egyptian setting, followed by an inner growling sound and intense fear. After leaving, she reports disturbed sleep and a sensation of pressure on her chest. Dadoosh interprets these experiences spiritually. Kimberly’s recollection does not, by itself, determine their cause.[^e08-guided-experience][^e08-nightmares]

Dadoosh also makes a qualification worth preserving: hypnosis does not necessarily involve demons. His discussion includes his own earlier attempts at hypnosis, rather than a controlled investigation of this particular experience.[^e08-hypnosis-practice]

### Allegations and the limits of the evidence

Kimberly says she entered a locked room and found objects and writings she considered alarming. Her description of jewellery is tentative: it looked to her like human bones. No forensic identification or independently inspected case file establishes that interpretation. The same limit applies to allegations about sacrifice: her account is not a report of witnessing a completed human sacrifice.[^e08-locked-room][^e08-halloween-allegation]

She also recounts unsuccessful exchanges with police. Near the end of the interview, however, she says police indicated that another complainant could help an investigation. Her testimony cannot establish that religious freedom gives a group general immunity from criminal investigation.[^e08-police]

Taking a distressed person seriously does not require turning every suspicion into an established fact. It requires accurate attribution, care with identifying details, and evidence appropriate to the allegation.

### Seeking help and reporting relief

Kimberly describes homelessness, difficulty being believed and a period of frightening experiences. The original interview does not establish a formal psychiatric diagnosis or a documented course of medication that failed. Those claims should not be added to her history.[^e08-homelessness][^e08-diagnosis]

She attributes relief to turning to God and, later, baptism. Her account also includes practical help from a church: shelter, food, fuel and companionship. The acute experiences are described over weeks; the much longer period she mentions concerns trying to explain what happened. These are different timelines.[^e08-church]

Her interpretation deserves faithful reporting. It is not a controlled comparison of treatments, nor does it establish why the improvement occurred. The episode’s reference to a *Fanjan* discussion about psychiatric diagnosis likewise does not demonstrate that persistent illness is caused by jinn.[^e08-fanjan]

Clinical assessment remains important. The National Institute of Mental Health describes several possible contributors to psychotic symptoms, including trauma, sleep deprivation and substance use, and explains that appropriate treatment can support recovery. This is general context, not a diagnosis of Kimberly.[^e08-nimh]

## A proposed demonic plan

In the final chapter, Dadoosh explicitly offers his personal interpretation: unseen beings may have exploited vulnerability and arranged the circumstances that brought Kimberly into the house. He connects this possibility with the qarin and spiritual deception. It should remain an attributed interpretation, rather than a reconstructed chain of events presented as known fact.[^e08-qarin-plan]

For a Muslim, the practical concern is how to seek Allah’s protection with discernment. Promises of hidden knowledge, flattering claims of spiritual distinction and frightening experiences do not establish anyone’s religious authority. Nor does reported relief establish that every practice associated with it is permissible. The Prophet ﷺ allowed ruqyah that contains no shirk, giving a textual basis for examining what is actually invoked.[^e08-ruqyah]

The episode closes by urging attention to spiritual needs and warning against harmful or fraudulent practitioners. Prayer, remembrance and lawful ruqyah belong within a life of trust in Allah. Compassionate support and appropriate medical care also matter; this testimony offers no basis for diagnosing a reader or abandoning treatment.[^e08-islamic-care][^e08-nimh]
